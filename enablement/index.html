Enablement

Teaching professionals to use AI well.

Most working adults have already tried these tools, usually without guidance and without a way to tell when the answer is wrong. The gap is not awareness. It is judgment: what to hand it, what to hold back, and how to check the result.

The pieces below are samples written for this page, in the short formats that adoption work actually uses. They show how I would explain things, not a program I have run at scale.

Sample 1 · One-page starter guide

One page, one role, real tasks.

A new user does not need a curriculum. They need five things to try on Monday, the short list of what never goes in, and a way to check the result.

Your first week with an AI assistant

A one-page starter guide for staff who handle applications, records and correspondence. Works with Copilot or Claude, whichever your organization has approved.

Five things to try on real work

  1. Turn a long thread into a to-do list.Read this email thread. List what each person still owes, with any dates mentioned. Say "not stated" where no date is given.
  2. Draft the reply you keep rewriting.Draft a reply that says no to the request, explains the rule in two sentences, and offers the next step. Polite, plain, under 120 words.
  3. Check a document against a checklist.Here is our checklist and here is the document. For each item say met, not met, or cannot tell, and quote the line you relied on.
  4. Explain a rule to someone new.Explain the policy below to a new colleague in plain language. Do not add anything that is not in the text.
  5. Turn meeting notes into a record.From these notes, write: decisions made, who does what by when, and open questions. Do not invent names or dates.

Never paste these in

  • Names with ID numbers, dates of birth, or passport and account numbers.
  • Health, immigration, disciplinary or financial details about a real person.
  • Anything marked confidential that your organization has not cleared for this tool.

Need help with a real case? Replace the person with "the applicant" and remove the numbers first. The assistant does not need them to help you write.

Check before you send

  1. Dates, amounts and names. Compare each one to the source yourself.
  2. Rules and citations. Open the rule and read the sentence. If you cannot find it, it does not go out.
  3. Your name is on it. If you would not sign it without the assistant, do not sign it with it.

Stop and ask when

  • The task decides something about a person: eligibility, status, money.
  • You are not sure the tool is approved for this kind of information.
  • The answer sounds confident and you cannot check it.

Why it is built this way

Every prompt is a task the reader already does. People adopt a tool when it removes a chore they recognize, not when they understand how it works.

What it leaves out

How the technology works, a feature tour, and anything that needs a second page. Those belong in a session, once the reader has a reason to care.

How I would adapt it

Same skeleton for each role family. The five tasks and the "never paste" list change with the role, and each version is tried by three people in that role before release.

Sample 2 · A guardrail in plain language

From the policy's sentence to what you do at your desk.

Governance teams write rules that are correct and hard to act on. Translating them is most of the job. The policy text here is typical wording, written for this example.

The policy says

"Personnel shall not input Confidential or Restricted Information into generative AI services that have not been approved for that data classification. Outputs must be reviewed by a qualified individual prior to use in any decision affecting an individual."

What that means for you

  • Use the assistant your organization gave you. Your personal account is a different tool with different rules, even if it looks the same.
  • Take the person out first. No names with ID numbers, no health, immigration or pay details.
  • You decide, it drafts. If the result affects someone's application, job or money, a person who knows the work reads it and owns it.
  • Not sure which kind of information you have? Treat it as confidential and ask. Asking takes a minute. Undoing a disclosure does not.

The translation keeps the rule's two obligations and drops its vocabulary. Policy owners review it before release so the plain version stays accurate.

Sample 3 · A 30-minute live session

"One task you will not do by hand again."

Short, hands-on, and built around the participant's own work. Everyone leaves with a prompt that worked on a real example.

ObjectiveEach person leaves with one saved, repeatable prompt for a task they do every week, tested on a real example from their own work.
0 to 5 minOne live demonstration on a task from this team, including the assistant getting something wrong and how I caught it.
5 to 10 minThe two rules: what never goes in, and what you check before it goes out.
10 to 25 minEveryone picks one weekly task and builds the prompt. I circulate. The usual fix is the same: the request left out who it is for and what good looks like.
25 to 30 minTwo people show theirs. Everyone saves their prompt where the team can find it.
One week laterA two-question check: did you use it again, and what got in the way? The answers decide the next session.

Why show a mistake

People trust a tool properly once they have watched it be wrong and seen how to catch it. A flawless demo teaches them to stop checking.

What I measure

Not attendance. Whether people used it again within a week, for what, and what stopped the ones who did not.

What keeps it going

The saved prompts become the team's shared examples, and the two people who presented become the ones colleagues ask first.

Sample 4 · Preparing a team for a new workflow

An application intake moved from email attachments to an automated flow.

A university office received application packets as email attachments and sorted them by hand. I designed the replacement and built it with Claude and Copilot, entirely inside the Microsoft 365 tools the office already had.

Before

  • Documents arrived across several emails, with whatever file names the applicant chose.
  • Staff saved, renamed and filed each one, then added up the funding by hand.
  • Missing items were found days later, when someone opened the folder.

After

  • One form, about a hundred questions, shown only as they apply to that applicant.
  • On submit: a folder is created, files are renamed to one standard, the funding is totaled, a summary is filed, and the tracker gets a row.
  • The applicant gets a confirmation and the office gets a notice with anything that needs attention flagged.
  1. What changesPackets arrive already sorted and totaled. Review starts at the summary, not at the inbox.
  2. Who does whatThe flow organizes. A staff member still reviews every document and still issues the official form by hand. Nothing is sent to the government system automatically.
  3. How to escalateA folder that looks incomplete goes to one named person, with the applicant's name and the date. Do not trust a green status over an empty folder.
  4. How success is measuredComplete packets on first submission, days from submission to review, and how often staff have to write back for a missing item.

Built with Microsoft Forms, Power Automate and SharePoint. Applicant records never leave the institution's own systems.

Course design

AI in the Workplace

Four two-hour sessions for working adults with no technical background. Not a coding class, and deliberately not "Intro to AI." Each participant leaves with one working workflow from their own job and one written rule for their own workplace.

1. What it is, and why it gets things wrong

A working mental model: it predicts text, it does not look things up. Why fluent wrong answers are most dangerous in your own field. Hands-on: one question, three tools, find the disagreements.

2. What you must never put in

Your obligations travel with the data. What happens to what you type. What to do when your employer has no rules yet. Hands-on: make a real document safe to share.

3. Making it useful

Which tasks it does reliably and which it quietly fails. Instructions as a specification. Giving it your own material to work from. Hands-on: build a repeatable prompt for one recurring task.

4. Checking the work

Verification habits that fit in the time you have. Which decisions are never delegated. When to say AI was used. Hands-on: audit an AI-written document against its source.

Why me for this

I learned it as a user, in work where mistakes count.

I am not a technologist explaining AI to office staff. I am office staff who taught myself to use it, in a field where a wrong date or a leaked record has real consequences. That is the position most colleagues are starting from, and it is why I can show them practical use without talking past them.

Before any of this I spent a decade in adult classrooms and years producing public radio, which is the craft of making a complicated thing clear to someone who has four minutes.
